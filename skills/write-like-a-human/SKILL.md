# Write like a human

Write prose that reflects real choices: what matters, what the evidence supports, and what this audience needs. Fix the thinking beneath a passage before polishing its surface.

## Ground rules

- Treat supposed AI tells as clues in combination, not banned words or proof of authorship.
- Preserve the author's meaning, facts, uncertainty, and level of confidence. Do not invent details to make the writing vivid.
- Match the genre and audience. A support reply, research summary, and personal essay should not share one generic "human" voice.
- Prefer a supplied voice sample over these defaults. Preserve its characteristic rhythm without copying phrases.
- Do not add typos, random slang, fake anecdotes, or awkwardness. Human writing is intentional, not artificially flawed.

## Workflow

1. Identify the purpose, audience, and requested voice. If none is given, use a direct, knowledgeable, unshowy voice.
2. Mark the claims that must survive the edit: facts, qualifications, quotations, citations, and calls to action.
3. Find the passage's real point. Replace generic framing with the concrete fact, observation, or decision that earns the space.
4. Rewrite for ordinary syntax and natural emphasis. Let sentence length and paragraph shape follow the ideas.
5. Remove scaffolding that belongs to a chatbot response rather than the artifact itself.
6. Check every factual claim and citation. Restore any nuance lost during tightening.
7. Read the result aloud. Repair lines that sound ceremonial, salesy, over-balanced, or suspiciously complete.
8. Return only the requested artifact unless the user asks for commentary.

## Make the content specific

- Lead with concrete nouns, dates, actions, mechanisms, and consequences.
- Cut claims that merely announce significance, legacy, impact, complexity, or connection to a broader trend.
- If something matters, explain how. Name the affected people, changed behavior, measurable result, or documented response.
- Remove superficial analysis appended with phrases such as "highlighting," "underscoring," "reflecting," or "contributing to." Keep the analysis only when evidence supports it and the reader needs it.
- Replace vague attribution such as "experts believe," "critics say," or "researchers note" with a named source and its actual claim. Otherwise, remove it.
- Do not turn one source or example into a consensus, trend, or non-exhaustive category.
- Avoid promotional language, travel-guide prose, and automatic praise. Describe what the subject has or does.
- Do not add a formulaic "challenges and future prospects" ending. End when the point is made.

## Use ordinary language

- Prefer `is`, `are`, and `has` when they say the thing cleanly. Do not inflate them into `serves as`, `stands as`, `boasts`, or `features` without a reason.
- Prefer familiar verbs: `wrote` over `authored`, `used` over `utilized`, `moved` over `relocated`, and `tried` over `attempted` when the meaning is the same.
- Watch for clusters of stock words such as `additionally`, `crucial`, `delve`, `enduring`, `foster`, `highlight`, `intricate`, `key`, `landscape`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry`, `testament`, `underscore`, and `vibrant`. Keep a word when it is exact; do not swap it mechanically for a synonym.
- Repeat the right noun when repetition is clearer than elegant variation.
- Use contractions, hedges, intensifiers, and occasional informality when the genre and voice call for them.
- Allow plain statements and uneven emphasis. Not every sentence needs a polished transition or a balanced cadence.

## Break formulaic patterns

- Do not default to sets of three. Use the number of examples or clauses the idea requires.
- Use contrasts such as "not only X, but Y" only when the contrast carries real meaning.
- Limit em dashes, parenthetical asides, and trailing participial phrases. They lose force when they become a house style.
- Vary sentence length for comprehension and emphasis, not by alternating lengths mechanically.
- Avoid false ranges such as "from X to Y" when X and Y are merely two related things.
- Keep conclusions proportional. Do not restate every point or finish with a vague optimistic summary.

## Keep the structure proportional

- Prefer paragraphs when a list would only chop continuous thought into labeled fragments.
- Use lists for genuinely parallel items, instructions, or reference material. Do not bold a mini-heading at the start of every bullet.
- Use sentence case for headings unless the requested style says otherwise.
- Avoid excessive headings, boldface, emoji, thematic breaks, and small tables that prose would handle better.
- Remove canned openings and handoffs such as "Of course," "Here is a breakdown," "I hope this helps," and "Let me know if you want more."
- Never leave planning notes, placeholders, instructions to the writer, knowledge-cutoff disclaimers, or chatbot markup in the finished text.

## Protect source integrity

- Support each citation-dependent sentence with a source that actually makes that claim.
- Open and verify links, titles, authors, dates, page numbers, DOI values, and ISBN values when tools or source material are available.
- Do not fabricate a citation, complete a partial reference from memory, or cite a general book without a relevant page when a page is needed for verification.
- Do not claim that information is private, scarce, undocumented, or unavailable merely because a search did not find it.
- Distinguish clearly between sourced fact, the author's interpretation, and uncertainty.
- Keep every edit explainable. If challenged, be able to point to the source, the brief, or a clear editorial reason.

## Compact examples

Instead of generic importance:

> The station serves as a vital transport hub, playing a pivotal role in the region's evolving economic landscape.

Write the supported fact:

> The station connects three regional rail lines and handled 2.4 million passengers in 2025.

Instead of vague analysis:

> The collection showcases the community's rich heritage, highlighting its enduring cultural significance.

Name what is present and why it matters:

> The collection contains 46 oral histories recorded from dockworkers between 1978 and 1983. Local schools now use them in lessons about the port's closure.

Do not invent the numbers or consequences in these patterns; use only facts available in the task's sources.

## Final pass

- Delete or rewrite any sentence that could apply unchanged to dozens of subjects.
- Replace abstractions with facts where the source permits.
- Remove unsupported praise, significance, consensus, and prediction.
- Check for repeated triads, negative parallelisms, sentence-opening transitions, and decorative `-ing` clauses.
- Remove formatting that does not help the reader navigate or compare information.
- Confirm that the voice is consistent without being uniformly polished.
- Confirm that the revision preserved meaning and did not strengthen the evidence.

The source is descriptive and Wikipedia-specific in places; apply its observations as editing heuristics, not universal rules or an authorship test.
