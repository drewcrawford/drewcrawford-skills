# Write like Hemingway

Use Hemingway as shorthand for disciplined prose, not as a costume. Make the writing easy to follow. Keep what matters. Cut what does not.

## Work in this order

1. Identify the purpose, audience, and facts the text must preserve.
2. Find the main point of each paragraph. Put it early unless suspense serves a clear purpose.
3. Replace abstract phrasing with concrete people, things, actions, and consequences.
4. Build sentences around strong nouns and verbs. Prefer active voice when the actor matters.
5. Cut throat-clearing, repetition, inflated transitions, decorative modifiers, and claims the evidence does not support.
6. Vary sentence length enough to keep the prose natural. Let short sentences carry weight.
7. Read the result aloud. Repair any line that sounds stiff, breathless, vague, or theatrical.
8. Return only the finished prose unless the user asks for notes or alternatives.

## Write plainly

- Prefer familiar, exact words over formal substitutes: `use` over `utilize`, `help` over `facilitate`, and `now` over `at this point in time`.
- Keep the subject and verb close together.
- State one clear action or claim before adding qualifications.
- Use concrete detail when the source provides it. Never invent detail for color.
- Use short paragraphs when the subject or action changes.
- Keep necessary technical, legal, or domain terms. Explain them simply when the audience may not know them.
- Use contractions when they fit the speaker and genre.
- End after the point lands. Do not attach a generic summary or lesson.

## Cut with care

- Delete openings such as "It is important to note," "In order to," and "It should be emphasized that" when the sentence works without them.
- Remove adjectives and adverbs that repeat what the noun or verb already says.
- Replace noun-heavy phrases with verbs: "made a decision" becomes "decided"; "conducted an investigation" becomes "investigated."
- Remove explanations the reader can infer, but keep the facts and logical steps needed to understand the argument.
- Keep hedges that express real uncertainty. Brevity must not turn a qualified claim into a certain one.
- Keep useful repetition. A repeated exact noun is often clearer than an elegant synonym.

## Avoid parody

- Do not add bullfights, war, whiskey, fishing, machismo, or melancholy merely to signal Hemingway.
- Do not copy recognizable phrases, passages, plots, characters, or verbal tics from his work.
- Do not make every sentence short. Unbroken staccato prose tires the reader and flattens emphasis.
- Do not string every clause together with `and`.
- Do not strip all adjectives, adverbs, figurative language, humor, or warmth. Keep what earns its place.
- Do not force modern business, technical, or personal writing into an old-fashioned voice.

## Protect the source

- Preserve facts, names, numbers, quotations, citations, uncertainty, and calls to action.
- Preserve the intended meaning and level of confidence.
- Follow the requested format and genre. A contract, API guide, condolence note, and short story need different kinds of clarity.
- Prefer accuracy over terseness and the user's established voice over a generic imitation.
- Flag a conflict only when the requested style would materially damage precision, tone, or meaning.

## Example

Instead of:

> Due to the fact that the storm was rapidly increasing in intensity, the crew made the decision to return back to the harbor.

Write:

> The storm grew stronger. The crew turned back to the harbor.

The revision keeps the cause and the action. It removes padding. It does not add drama the source did not earn.

## Final pass

- Can the reader understand each sentence on the first pass?
- Does each paragraph do one job?
- Are the nouns concrete and the verbs doing real work?
- Can any word go without losing meaning, rhythm, or necessary tone?
- Did the edit preserve every fact and qualification?
- Does the prose sound direct rather than like an imitation?
